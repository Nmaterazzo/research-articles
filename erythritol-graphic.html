Erythritol:
Safe Sweetener or
Hidden Danger?

In 2023, a Cleveland Clinic study made headlines linking erythritol to blood clots and cardiovascular events. The fearmongering spread fast. The actual evidence tells a different story, built on reverse causation, a 10-person study, and a fundamental misunderstanding of what high plasma erythritol actually means in the body.

01
What Erythritol Actually Is and How Your Body Handles It
Bornet et al. Regul Toxicol Pharmacol 1996 · EFSA Safety Opinion 2015 · Munro et al. pharmacokinetics · Khafagy et al. Diabetes 2024

Erythritol is a four-carbon sugar alcohol found naturally in fruits, mushrooms, and fermented foods. It is also commercially produced by fermenting glucose with yeast. What sets it apart from other sweeteners and sugar alcohols is how the body handles it: almost nothing happens metabolically, which is both why it is useful as a sweetener and why the panic about it misses something fundamental.

The Cephalic Phase Insulin Response: Does Erythritol Trigger It?
Pullicin et al. 2021 · Smeets EASD 2019 · Lasschuijt et al. 2017 · CPIR literature review
The CPIR concern that follows non-nutritive sweeteners broadly does not apply to erythritol. It produces no cephalic phase insulin response, no blood glucose rise, and no caloric yield, while still triggering satiety hormones. This makes it one of the metabolically cleanest sweeteners that exists.
02
The 1,000-Fold Plasma Spike: Dramatic Number, Important Context
Witkowski et al. Nat Med 2023 · Bornet et al. Regul Toxicol Pharmacol 1996 · Khafagy et al. Diabetes 2024 · pharmacokinetics studies

Erythritol's baseline plasma concentration is very low because your body does not make much of it under normal conditions. When you eat a large dose, the plasma level spikes dramatically in relative terms. This number was central to the scaremongering. What it actually means requires understanding what baseline represents and how quickly the spike resolves.

Plasma Erythritol After a 30g Dose: The Spike and Recovery
Witkowski et al. 2023 pharmacokinetics data · n=8 healthy volunteers · 30g oral dose in beverage · LC-MS/MS measurement

Baseline / recovered
The "1,000-fold" spike sounds alarming but context matters. The baseline is extremely low (~3.75 µmol/L) because you normally have almost no dietary erythritol and only trace endogenous production. The return to baseline within 48 hours reflects efficient renal clearance. The spike from diet is fundamentally different from the persistent elevation seen in metabolically sick patients in the Witkowski observational cohort.
Why the Spike Number Doesn't Mean What Headlines Said The platelet reactivity thresholds used in Witkowski's in vitro experiments were 4.5 to 45 µmol/L. The actual plasma level after eating 30g reaches roughly 6,480 µmol/L. So the in vitro platelet studies were done at concentrations roughly 14 to 1,400 times lower than what actually happens in plasma after eating erythritol. The in vitro concentrations were chosen to match fasting levels in their sick cohort, not what happens after dietary ingestion. This is a critical mismatch that goes largely unreported.
03
The Witkowski Studies: Dissecting the Evidence Chain
Witkowski et al. Nat Med 2023 · Witkowski et al. ATVB 2024 · independent critiques · MDPI Nutrients review 2023

The entire erythritol scare comes from one research group at the Cleveland Clinic. Three studies, same team, same hypothesis. Understanding each study's actual design is essential because each one has a different type of weakness, and together they were presented as a convergent line of evidence when they actually share the same foundational flaw.

The Witkowski Evidence Chain: What Each Study Actually Did
Study 1: Witkowski Nat Med 2023 · Study 2: Witkowski ATVB 2024 · Study 3: additional platelet mechanistic work · all from same Cleveland Clinic group
The 5-point breakdown (Study 2)
01Tiny sample size. n=10 per group. No statistical power for clinical conclusions.
02Acute response only. Single blood draw at 30 minutes. No follow-up to track resolution.
03Unrealistic dose. 30g in one sitting equals 3-4 product servings consumed at once.
04Platelet reactivity is not thrombosis. Enhanced reactivity may increase risk theoretically, but is not proven here.
05Same research group as Study 1. Same lead authors, same lab, no independent replication.
The in vitro concentration mismatch
The platelet assays in the lab used erythritol concentrations of 4.5 to 45 µmol/L to model the fasting levels in sick patients. But after actually eating 30g of erythritol, plasma reaches ~6,480 µmol/L. The in vitro experiments modeled a concentration 100 to 1,000 times lower than what happens in real humans who eat erythritol. If anything, the in vitro platelet effects at those low concentrations are hard to interpret as a dietary risk.
04
The Firefighter Fallacy: Reverse Causation and Confounding by Indication
Frontiers Nutrition 2022 · Hootman et al. PPP biology · Khafagy et al. Diabetes 2024 · MDPI Nutrients review 2023

This is the core of the debunk. There are formal names for what went wrong in the Witkowski observational study, and they stack on top of each other to explain why the findings were almost guaranteed to show an association even if erythritol is completely harmless.

The Pentose Phosphate Pathway: Why Sick People Have High Erythritol
Frontiers Nutrition 2022 · Hootman et al. · bioRxiv 2022 · PPP biology and erythritol endogenous synthesis
Reverse Causation
The most precise term for what happened. The metabolic disease caused the elevated erythritol levels, not the other way around. The causal arrow is backwards. This is why Khafagy ran a bidirectional Mendelian randomization specifically designed to test which direction causation runs. The MR found erythritol does not increase CAD risk, but disease processes may increase erythritol.
Confounding by Indication
The clinical epidemiology term for the cohort selection problem. Patients were enrolled because they were undergoing cardiac risk assessment. Their illness drove both the enrollment and the elevated erythritol. Any biomarker you measure in this cohort will look like a CVD risk factor because the cohort was selected for CVD risk. This makes the association almost inevitable regardless of whether erythritol does anything.
The Firefighter Fallacy
The biological version. The PPP upregulates specifically as an antioxidant response to counteract oxidative stress. Erythritol itself may even have antioxidant properties. Blaming erythritol for the damage caused by the metabolic dysfunction that elevated it is like blaming firefighters for the fire because they always show up when there's a fire. The firemen and the fire are correlated. The firemen did not cause the fire.
The Natural Experiment That Kills the Hypothesis
People with inborn errors of pentose phosphate metabolism have chronically elevated erythritol their entire lives. If high erythritol caused thrombosis and CVD, this population would show it clearly. They do not. This is a clean natural experiment and represents the strongest counter-evidence to the Witkowski causal claim.
05
Mendelian Randomization: The Closest Thing to a Causal Test
Khafagy et al. Diabetes 2024 · Zhang et al. PMC 2025 · JACC Advances 2025 · bidirectional MR design

Mendelian randomization uses genetic variants that naturally predict erythritol levels as a proxy for a randomized experiment, bypassing the lifestyle confounding that plagues observational data. Two MR studies exist. They conflict somewhat, and understanding both is important for an honest account of the evidence.

Mendelian Randomization Results: Erythritol and Cardiometabolic Outcomes
Khafagy et al. Diabetes 2024 (bidirectional MR, European ancestry) · Zhang et al. 2025 (two-sample MR) · point estimates with 95% CI
Null result (CI crosses 1.0)
Possible benefit (BMI reduction)

The Khafagy 2024 bidirectional MR is the stronger study. Bidirectional design allows testing which direction causation runs. Zhang 2025 found ORs of 1.001 to 1.046 — tiny effects that may reflect instrument pleiotropy. The balance of MR evidence does not support the cardiovascular alarm.
The BMI Finding Deserves More Attention The Khafagy MR found that genetically predicted higher erythritol is associated with lower BMI. This is consistent with erythritol's role as a zero-calorie sugar replacement — using it instead of sugar reduces overall caloric intake. If anything, the causal direction appears beneficial for metabolic outcomes, not harmful. This finding has received almost no coverage compared to the platelet story.
06
What the Counter-Evidence Actually Shows
Flint et al. Acta Diabetol 2014 · Hootman et al. 2017 · Livesey et al. · vascular RCTs · inborn errors of metabolism data
RCT in type 2 diabetics: improved endothelial function
Flint et al. gave erythritol daily to patients with type 2 diabetes over 4 weeks and found it acutely improved small-vessel endothelial function and reduced central aortic stiffness. This is the population the Witkowski alarm was most directed at. Actual dietary erythritol moved the dials in the beneficial direction.
RCT in obese adults: no worsening of vascular function
A separate trial in people with obesity — another high-risk group — found that 5 weeks of erythritol supplementation did not worsen vascular function on any measured parameter. Zero negative vascular signal in the population supposedly most at risk.
Inborn error patients: lifetime high levels, no excess thrombosis
People with genetic defects in pentose phosphate metabolism have persistently elevated erythritol throughout their entire lives. If elevated erythritol caused platelet hyperreactivity and thrombosis, this population would be a clean natural experiment showing it. They show no excess thrombosis or cardiovascular events attributable to erythritol.
Satiety hormones: surprisingly favorable profile
Despite providing zero calories, erythritol triggers GLP-1, CCK, and PYY release at levels similar to glucose and suppresses ghrelin. This means it provides a satiety signal without the glycemic hit. For weight management in metabolically compromised individuals, this profile is genuinely useful and goes completely unreported in the panic coverage.
07
Claim by Claim: What the Evidence Actually Supports
Erythritol causes blood clots and cardiovascular events
Not causally supported
Observational association is real but explained by reverse causation. High plasma erythritol in cardiac patients reflects endogenous PPP upregulation from metabolic dysfunction, not dietary intake. MR finds no causal link to CAD. Inborn error patients with lifetime elevated erythritol show no excess thrombosis.
The platelet reactivity study proves erythritol is dangerous
Overreach from a weak study
n=10 per group. Single acute measurement at 30 minutes. 30g single-sitting dose. Platelet reactivity does not equal observed thrombosis. Same research group as the original alarm. No independent replication. The in vitro concentrations used were far below actual post-ingestion plasma levels.
Daily erythritol consumption damages your cardiovascular system
Not supported by controlled studies
Two RCTs in high-risk populations (T2D and obese adults) found no worsening of vascular function. One found improved endothelial function and reduced aortic stiffness after 4 weeks of daily erythritol in diabetics.
Erythritol raises blood sugar or insulin
False — well established
Glycemic index 0. Insulinemic index 2. No change in blood glucose or insulin in studies ranging from 20 to 75g doses. It is not metabolized and passes through the bloodstream unchanged before renal excretion.
Erythritol triggers the cephalic phase insulin response
No — not observed
CPIR has been documented for sucrose and in some subsets for sucralose, but not for erythritol. The sweet taste does not appear to trigger a pancreatic insulin pre-release, which is one of erythritol's advantages over other sweeteners.
High plasma erythritol is a useful biomarker
Yes, but as a marker — not a cause
Fasting plasma erythritol does correlate with metabolic dysfunction and CVD risk — because it reflects PPP activity, which increases under oxidative stress. It is a valid marker of metabolic health status. It is not a cause of the conditions it marks, and dietary erythritol does not produce the same persistent elevations as endogenous production.
Synthesis: What Actually Happened Here
The erythritol scare was a case study in how compelling-sounding observational data, combined with supportive in vitro and animal work, can generate alarm that the actual causal evidence doesn't support. The Witkowski group did real science. The methodological problems are not incompetence. They are inherent to the study designs chosen.

The observational flaw was structural. You cannot measure fasting plasma erythritol in people undergoing cardiac risk assessment and conclude that erythritol caused their cardiac risk. Their elevated endogenous erythritol is a predictable consequence of the metabolic dysfunction that brought them to the clinic. This is textbook reverse causation compounded by confounding by indication. The PPP biology explains it completely: overactive PPP under oxidative stress produces erythritol as a byproduct, and erythritol itself may even be part of the antioxidant defense response. You are blaming the firemen for the fire.

The intervention study was too small to draw conclusions from. Ten people per group, single acute measurement, unrealistic dose, same research group. It adds a data point but cannot bear the clinical weight placed on it.

The counter-evidence points in a different direction. RCTs in diabetics and obese adults find no vascular harm and even some improvement. The Mendelian randomization finds no causal CAD link and suggests erythritol may reduce BMI. People born with permanently elevated erythritol do not clot to death. These are not small footnotes. They are the kinds of evidence you would expect to see if something were truly harmful, and they are absent.

The bottom line on dietary erythritol at normal consumption levels: zero glycemic impact, no insulin response, no CPIR, favorable satiety hormone profile, no evidence of vascular harm in controlled trials, and no causal CVD link in the best available genetic evidence. The fearmongering was built on sick people's endogenous metabolism being misattributed to a sweetener.
